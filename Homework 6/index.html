Homework: Statistical Independence

Bivariate dataset (X, F): contingency table, marginal and conditional frequencies, expected frequencies, chi-square test and interpretation.

1. Theoretical background

Two events A and B are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B), equivalently P(A | B) = P(A). Knowing one outcome does not change the probability of the other.

For two categorical variables X (r categories) and F (c categories), independence means that for every cell (i, j) the joint relative frequency equals the product of the marginals:

fij = fi· · f·j  ⇔  nij = Eij = (ni· · n·j) / n

Equivalently, all conditional distributions of F given X = xi are identical to the marginal distribution of F. The deviation from this ideal is measured by Pearson’s statistic:

χ² = Σi,j (nij − Eij)² / Eij,   df = (r − 1)(c − 1)

Under H0 (independence) χ² approximately follows a chi-square distribution with df degrees of freedom (reliable when all Eij ≥ 5). The p-value is P(χ²df ≥ χ²obs); if p < α we reject H0. Cramér’s V = √(χ² / (n·min(r−1, c−1))) measures the strength of the association (0 = none, 1 = perfect).

6. Interpretation

7. Association is not causation

A significant chi-square only says that the data are unlikely under independence: the variables are statistically associated. It does not say that X causes F, nor in which direction an effect would run. The association may be produced by a confounding variable (a third factor influencing both), by reverse causation, or by selection effects or chance. Conversely, failing to reject H0 does not prove independence; it may reflect a small sample and low power. Moreover, with very large n even trivial associations become “significant”, so the effect size (Cramér’s V) should be reported alongside the p-value. Establishing causality requires a suitable design (e.g. randomised experiment) or explicit causal assumptions, not a test of independence alone.
