Part 1 – Modes of Convergence

Let X₁, X₂, … be random variables on the same probability space and X a random variable (possibly a constant). The four main modes differ in what kind of closeness between Xₙ and X they require.

ModeDefinitionMeaning
In probability (Xₙ →ᵖ X)∀ε>0: P(|Xₙ−X|>ε) → 0The probability of a “large” deviation vanishes; for each n the deviation can still happen.
Almost surely (Xₙ →ᵃˢ X)P(lim Xₙ = X) = 1Along almost every single trajectory the sequence converges; deviations eventually stop occurring.
Mean-square / L² (Xₙ →ᴸ² X)E[(Xₙ−X)²] → 0Average squared error vanishes; requires finite second moments.
In distribution (Xₙ →ᵈ X)Fₙ(x) → F(x) at every continuity point x of FOnly the laws converge; Xₙ and X need not even live on the same space.

Relationships

a.s. ⟹ in probability ⟹ in distribution    and    L² ⟹ in probability
  • L² ⟹ probability by Chebyshev/Markov: P(|Xₙ−X|>ε) ≤ E[(Xₙ−X)²]/ε².
  • a.s. ⟹ probability by dominated convergence of indicator functions. The converse fails: the “typewriter” sequence (indicators of intervals sweeping [0,1] with shrinking length) converges to 0 in probability and L² but not a.s.
  • Probability ⟹ distribution; the converse fails in general (Xₙ = −X for symmetric X has the same law but does not converge to X). It holds when the limit is a constant.
  • L² and a.s. do not imply each other (a.s. needs a dominating bound, e.g. Xₙ = n·1{U<1/n} → 0 a.s. but E[Xₙ²] = n → ∞).
  • Partial converse: convergence in probability implies a.s. convergence along a subsequence.

Role in the Law of Large Numbers (LLN)

Let X̄ₙ = (X₁+…+Xₙ)/n with i.i.d. X_i, mean μ and variance σ².

  • Weak LLN (WLLN): X̄ₙ →ᵖ μ. With finite variance the proof is one line: Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²/n, hence P(|X̄ₙ−μ|>ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²) → 0. In fact this also shows L² convergence, since E[(X̄ₙ−μ)²] = σ²/n → 0.
  • Strong LLN (SLLN) (Kolmogorov): if E|X₁| < ∞, then X̄ₙ →ᵃˢ μ. This is the statement about one single, long experiment: the running average of that sequence settles at μ. It is the theoretical justification of Monte Carlo simulation and of the frequentist interpretation of probability.

Statistical meaning: convergence in probability of an estimator to the true parameter is exactly consistency (weak); a.s. convergence is strong consistency.

Role in the Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

The LLN says X̄ₙ collapses to the constant μ; the CLT describes the shape of the fluctuations around μ. If 0<σ²<∞:

√n (X̄ₙ − μ) / σ  →ᵈ  N(0,1)

Only convergence in distribution can hold here: the standardized sums do not converge to any random variable in probability or a.s. (they keep oscillating), but their distribution functions converge. Related results using this mode: Slutsky’s theorem, the continuous mapping theorem, the delta method, and asymptotic confidence intervals and tests (e.g. Wald tests), which use the CLT together with consistency (→ᵖ) of variance estimators.

Why does √n appear?

  • Additivity of variance under independence. For independent (or just uncorrelated) variables Var(ΣXᵢ) = ΣVar(Xᵢ) = nσ². Hence sd(Sₙ) = σ√n, and sd(X̄ₙ) = σ√n / n = σ/√n. Without independence there are covariance terms, Var(Sₙ) = nσ² + ΣΣ Cov(Xᵢ,Xⱼ), and the rate can change (e.g. perfectly correlated variables give variance n²σ², no averaging gain at all).
  • Right scaling. Sₙ − nμ has standard deviation of order √n: it grows (so dividing by n kills it: LLN) but slower than n. Multiplying X̄ₙ−μ by exactly √n keeps the variance constant at σ² and gives a non-degenerate limit (CLT). Multiplying by less gives 0, by more gives ∞.
  • Statistical inference. The standard error σ/√n implies that halving the error costs four times the data. Estimators are “√n-consistent”: θ̂ₙ − θ = O_p(1/√n), and √n(θ̂ₙ−θ) →ᵈ N(0, V) for MLEs with V = 1/I(θ) (Fisher information).

Part 2 – Conditional Probability, Bayes’ Theorem and Inference

Conditional probability

For events A, B with P(B)>0: P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B): the probability of A after restricting the sample space to B. Events are independent iff P(A|B) = P(A). The law of total probability P(B) = Σᵢ P(B|Aᵢ)P(Aᵢ) over a partition {Aᵢ} links marginal and conditional quantities. In statistics, conditional distributions underlie regression (Y|X), the likelihood (data conditional on the parameter), and sufficiency.

Bayes’ theorem and the three components

p(θ | data) = p(data | θ) · p(θ) / p(data),   p(data) = ∫ p(data|θ)p(θ)dθ
  • Prior p(θ): beliefs about the parameter before seeing the data (expert knowledge, earlier studies, or weakly informative/“flat” choices).
  • Likelihood p(data|θ): how probable the observed data are for each θ, viewed as a function of θ. It is not a probability distribution over θ.
  • Posterior p(θ|data): updated beliefs, ∝ likelihood × prior. The denominator (marginal likelihood/evidence) is just a normalizing constant.

The posterior of today is the prior of tomorrow: Bayesian updating is sequential and coherent, processing data in batches or all at once gives the same result. All inference (point estimates such as posterior mean/mode, credible intervals, predictive distributions, hypothesis comparison through Bayes factors) is derived from the posterior. Contrary to frequentist inference, one can say directly “P(θ∈[a,b] | data) = 0.95”.

Example 1 – Diagnostic test (discrete prior)

Disease prevalence (prior), sensitivity P(+|D), specificity P(−|no D). Compute P(D|+).


Even with a good test, a low prior (rare disease) yields a modest posterior: ignoring the prior (the “base rate fallacy”) is a classic error and shows the influence of the prior on conclusions.

Example 2 – Beta–Binomial model (continuous prior)

Data: k successes in n Bernoulli(θ) trials. Likelihood ∝ θᵏ(1−θ)ⁿ⁻ᵏ. With prior θ ~ Beta(a,b) the posterior is conjugate: Beta(a+k, b+n−k), with mean (a+k)/(a+b+n), a weighted average of prior mean a/(a+b) and sample proportion k/n; the weight on the data grows with n.


Try: (1) a=b=1 (flat prior): posterior ≈ likelihood. (2) a=b=25 (strong prior at 0.5) with few data: the prior dominates. (3) increase n with k/n fixed: posteriors from different priors merge (the likelihood wins).

Advantages and limitations

AdvantagesLimitations
Incorporates prior knowledge; works well with small samples.Prior choice is subjective and can bias conclusions, especially with little data.
Direct probability statements about parameters; natural handling of uncertainty and prediction.Posterior often has no closed form: needs MCMC/variational methods (computational cost).
Sequential updating; hierarchical modelling.“Non-informative” priors are not unique and are not invariant under reparametrization; sensitivity analysis is needed.
Bernstein–von Mises: with enough data posterior ≈ N(θ̂, 1/(nI(θ))) and the prior washes out, linking Bayesian and frequentist inference through the same √n rate.Misspecified likelihood leads to misleading posteriors, as in any approach.

Summary: LLN (→ᵖ, →ᵃˢ, →ᴸ²) tells where estimators go; the CLT (→ᵈ) tells how they fluctuate, at rate 1/√n thanks to additive variance under independence; Bayes’ theorem combines prior and likelihood into a posterior whose dependence on the prior fades at that same rate.
