3. Comparison across values of n

Comparison of the three quantities

Total score Sₙ. Its mean is nμ and its variance is nσ², so it spreads out without bound as n grows. In the Bernoulli case the whole distribution drifts to the right at speed μ per system and widens like √n; in the Rademacher case it stays centered at 0 and only widens. Histograms of Sₙ for different n therefore cannot be compared on the same axis: they have no limit.

Average score Sₙ/n. Its variance σ²/n goes to 0, so the histogram shrinks to a spike at μ (p in the Bernoulli case, 0 in the Rademacher case). This is the Law of Large Numbers in action: P(|Sₙ/n − μ| > ε) falls toward 0 as n increases, and the empirical frequencies in the table are expected to lie below the Chebyshev bound σ²/(nε²), up to finite-sample fluctuations; the bound is conservative (the true decay is much faster, in fact exponential, by Hoeffding). The price of this normalization is that all the randomness is squeezed out: the limit is a constant and carries no information about fluctuations.

Normalized score Sₙ/√n. Its variance is exactly σ² for every n, so the spread neither explodes nor vanishes. In the Rademacher case the mean is 0 and the histogram is already close to N(0,1) for small n, with the KS distance generally decreasing as n grows. Its observed value has two sources: the finite-n approximation error (Sₙ/√n is discrete, the normal is continuous) and the sampling error from only m attackers, of order 1/√m; it therefore need not reach 1/√m. In the Bernoulli case the mean is √n·p, so the histogram translates to the right as n increases and only the centered version (Sₙ − np)/√(np(1−p)) converges to N(0,1); a small residual asymmetry at small n (skewness (1−2p)/√(np(1−p))) explains why Bernoulli converges more slowly than the symmetric Rademacher case, especially for p far from 1/2.

Summary. The three scalings answer different questions: Sₙ describes the absolute size of the aggregate score (diverging), Sₙ/n its per-system rate (deterministic in the limit), and Sₙ/√n the typical size of fluctuations around the mean (a Gaussian, with scale σ).

4. Interpretation

Meaning of n and m. Each attacker i = 1,…,m faces the same n systems in sequence; Xⱼ is the penetration score obtained on the j-th system and Sₙ is the total damage accumulated by that attacker. Different attackers are independent, so the m final values of Sₙ are an i.i.d. sample from the distribution of Sₙ, and the m trajectories in section 1 are m realizations of the same random walk.

Effect of the system size n. In the Bernoulli model (each system is breached with probability p) the expected number of breached systems is np, with fluctuations of order √(np(1−p)). Relative to its own size, the attack becomes more predictable as n grows: the fraction of breached systems Sₙ/n concentrates on p, so for a large infrastructure all attackers end up with essentially the same success rate and the outcome is nearly deterministic. In absolute terms, however, the uncertainty on the number of compromised systems still grows like √n. In the Rademacher model, where gains and losses cancel on average, the aggregate score has no drift: the typical magnitude is √n, reached only through random excursions, and the final score is symmetric around zero: positive and negative scores are equally likely, although a zero score is also possible when n is even.

Effect of the number of attackers m. m does not change the law of Sₙ for a single attacker. It controls how well we observe that law: the empirical histograms, variances and exceedance probabilities have sampling error of order 1/√m. It also controls the extremes: among m attackers the best one reaches, under a normal (sub-Gaussian) approximation, roughly nμ + σ√(2n ln m), so adding attackers raises the worst case for the defender only logarithmically, while the average case is unchanged.

Practical reading. For a defender, the LLN says that on a large system the average damage per system is predictable (p), so it can be budgeted. The CLT says how much margin is needed around that prediction: about σ√n standard deviations, i.e. a relative margin of order 1/√n, which makes large systems proportionally safer to forecast but not absolutely safer. For an attack that is symmetric (Rademacher) there is no systematic advantage for either side, and any large positive score is just a fluctuation of size √n. Note that the Rademacher model is an abstract model of gains and losses, not a literal count of penetrated systems; only the Bernoulli model counts compromised systems directly.
